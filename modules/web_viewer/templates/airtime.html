Airtime

This is modelled airtime, not a measurement of the channel being busy. Over serial, BLE or TCP the bot is a client of a radio: it cannot see whether the channel was clear, only how long a packet of a given size occupies it at the current SF/BW/coding rate. Real occupancy (CAD, LBT) belongs to a radio driver, and a repeater's own occupancy comes from that repeater's API. The two shares are kept apart on purpose: rx is what this node hears — a local observation, not the airtime of the whole channel — and tx is what the bot spends itself. Every heard copy is counted, flood repetitions included, because on one shared channel each repetition is its own transmission: a share above 100% is a fact about parallel traffic (each radio senses the carrier near itself, not across the mesh), not an overflow. ≥ channel is the floor for the whole shared medium — heard copies plus the shallower copies each flood tree proves — and it is a floor, never a ceiling.

A gap is not a zero: a period with no packet is absent from the result, so the line breaks there rather than dropping to 0. A bucket that was observed and held nothing on air stays a zero — those are two different facts, and a chart that draws the first as the second shows a cliff that reads as an outage. All figures and the chart speak percent: the rx/tx figures are percent of the time buckets were actually written (unwritten periods stay unknown, not silent), p50/p95 are the nearest-rank occupancy of a single bucket, and each column of the chart is the occupancy of its own span. The Copies figure is the share of the identity-covered airtime the flood spent on repetitions after the first copy this radio heard — a dash means the period predates that split, not a flood-free channel. The line under the chart compares this period with the equal one before it, and states a change only when both periods hold recorded buckets; hover on a column gives its own copy share and the change against the column one period back, when the two line up.

Senders by airtime

Sender Airtime Packets Advert Last seen

Ranked by airtime, not by packet count: a talker sending many small packets can cost more than one sending a long message. Every heard copy counts, flood repetitions included, and the traffic this radio cannot trace to a sender sits in the unattributed row — the table therefore adds up to the whole heard channel, and its total can exceed the wall-clock span. Attributed senders are the ones whose packets carry their identity: adverts, which name their advertiser in the clear, the bot itself, and messages this radio's own decryption names an author for — copies not yet written to the table move out of the unknown then, never rows already written; the write holds a tracked copy for sixty seconds so a late decryption can still claim it. Open channels name no author at all: the radio opens them with a shared key and reports none, so that anonymity is structural, not a gap in this measurement. Traffic this radio cannot decrypt stays unknown. The Advert column is the share of that sender's airtime spent on adverts — the presence traffic every MeshCore node broadcasts — and a dash means the share is unknown because that sender predates the split. The table follows the history period selected above: each row sums that station's day rows inside the window, aligned to whole UTC days, so a small number here is not a quiet station — absence in this table is not silence on the channel. The day-by-day detail reaches back only to when this breakdown was introduced, so a long window understates traffic older than that; the station's lifetime totals stay on its detail page. Emitters by airtime

Emitter Airtime Packets Advert Last seen

Who occupies the channel, relay repeaters included: each heard copy is charged to the station that physically transmitted it, named on the wire by MeshCore's flood path, whose last entry is that station. A 4- or 6-digit label is a node address, the first bytes of a relay key, shown as a name only when exactly one known contact matches it. This table and the senders table above hold the same copies once per fact — carrier and emitter are two different stations — so they must never be summed. Like the senders table, this one covers the history period selected above, aligned to whole UTC days, and its day detail reaches back only to when this breakdown was introduced. MeshCore writes a relay's node address 2 bytes wide in short packets and 3 bytes wide in extended ones, so one relay can stand under two labels; when the ledger knows only one longer address under a shorter one, the short copies are folded into that row and the cell names the labels it merged. Where the ledger knows two longer addresses, the short row stays on its own: the fold would pick a station the wire did not name. Couvert par la période choisie.

Entry relays by airtime

Entry relay Airtime Packets Advert Last seen

Where the relayed copies entered this mesh: MeshCore's flood path names the hops a copy passed through, and its FIRST entry is the first relay that repeated it — the gateway that carried the copy to this radio's side of the network. It is not the author: a multicast names its writer nowhere on the wire, and the writer's own antenna usually sits behind this first hop, not equal to it. It is not the emitter either: the emitter table above reads the path from its other end, the last hop that carried the copy here. Copies heard directly passed through no relay and belong to no row here, so this table is deliberately smaller than the other two; all three hold the same copies once per fact and must never be summed. The labels are node addresses — the first bytes of a relay key — shown as a name only when exactly one known contact matches; when the ledger knows only one longer address under a shorter one, the short copies fold into that row and the cell names the labels it merged. Like the other tables, this one covers the history period selected above, aligned to whole UTC days. Les copies reçues en direct n'entrent pas ici, et les trois tableaux ne se cumulent jamais.
