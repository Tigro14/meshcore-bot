    Channel occupancy

    Baliz: received copies and RF roles

    The passive /api/telemetry/occupancy/analysis?window=1h|24h|7d model retains schema version 1 and advances its method version to 6. It adds receptions, computed from existing metadata; there is no migration, new radio transmission, or retention change. The separation of writer, first flood relay and heard emitter follows the questions raised by Tigro's Airtime work, while keeping Baliz's full-frame costing, 1/2/3-byte paths and existing reconstruction model.

    • repetition_factor is received copies divided by distinct payload fingerprints.
    • additional_received_airtime_percent is the airtime of receptions after the first local reception of each fingerprint, divided by total received airtime. The first reception is selected inside the requested period; it need not be the original transmitter. Each copy uses its own complete frame length and recorded modulation. Useful flood forwarding is included; this is neither a waste percentage nor a global network repetition rate. Unknown short hashes do not collapse identities.
    • The same weighted share is provided in receptions.series and the existing chart bins. A bin can contain only additional copies of a packet first received in an earlier bin of the selected period. Missing reception history remains unknown.
    • Truncated queries expose status: truncated and null ratio/weighted-share fields. A period with no receptions has no repetition factor or airtime share, not a measured zero. These metrics apply to retained normalized RF frames, not undecodable RF energy.
    • receptions.roles.emitters attributes each eligible received flood copy to its last relay; entry_relays attributes it to its first relay. Both contain rx_airtime_ms, received_packets and message/advert/other counts. No-path flood copies with a known author can name their direct emitter and have no entry relay. Direct-route paths and TRACE never establish either relay role. Unresolved prefixes stay separate labels; widths 1, 2 and 3 resolve only to a unique known repeater/room server. Unknown emitters are explicit, and emitter rows plus that bucket reconcile with local RX airtime.
    • Authors retain distinct-packet counts and reconstructed source-plus-relay radio cost. They are ranked by that cost, with unknown costs last and a reconstruction-coverage field. Author cost and received-copy role totals must never be summed.
    • receptions.comparison compares the preceding equal-length period. Changes in received airtime, distinct packets and additional-copy airtime share are supplied only if both windows have confirmed continuous listening and untruncated, nonempty observations. The pair ends at the latest fresh confirmed listening timestamp (at most 75 seconds behind the request), exposed as end_time; this excludes the writer's unconfirmed tail without bridging any internal outage. The page's selected-period totals still end at the request time, and the comparison carries its own current/previous metrics. Otherwise previous observations can be shown, but change fields are null. No automatic congestion classification or alert is inferred.

    The page keeps four header cards and one combined chart. The chart summary reports distinct packets, receptions and additional-copy airtime; hover details show the same breakdown per bin. The existing Sources detail table switches between the three roles. Per-repeater reconstructed and scenario duty cycles remain separate from these RX metrics.

    This feature counts airtime: how much of the channel a packet takes, and how much the bot itself takes. It is off by default.

    What it is, and what it is not

    The bot is a client of a radio, not a controller of one. Over serial, BLE or TCP it cannot see whether the channel was busy, so nothing here measures occupancy in the radio-driver sense — CAD, LBT, or a clear-channel assessment. Those belong to a repeater or a modem, and a repeater's real occupancy comes from that repeater's own API.

    What this does is compute how long a packet of a given size occupies the channel at a given modulation, using the Semtech AN1200.13 expression:

    symbol_time = 2^SF / BW
airtime     = (preamble + 4.25 + symbols) * symbol_time
symbols     = 8 + ceil((8*PL - 4*SF + 28 + CRC - header) / (4*(SF - 2*DE))) * CR

    The 4.25 is the sync pattern, which exists even with a zero-length preamble; +16 is the CRC bits; -20 is the difference between implicit and explicit header mode; CR is the coding-rate denominator (5..8). Low-data-rate optimization is applied by the datasheet's own rule (symbol time ≥ 16.38 ms) unless the configuration forces it.

    So a percentage from this page is a modelled channel load, correct for the modulation it is given, and wrong whenever the modulation is wrong. That is why the device's reported SF/BW/CR win over the configured ones: a bot configured for SF9 on a mesh running SF11 reports an airtime no packet on the channel has ever had.

    rx share and tx share are never merged

    Share What it counts Why it cannot stand for "the channel"
    rx airtime the packets the node hears would take A node only hears part of the channel: hidden nodes, antenna gain, spatial reuse. It is a local observation.
    tx airtime the bot sends The only part the bot can decide about. Counted at the moment the bot asks the radio to send, on the message body alone.

    A single combined percentage would present one node's hearing as the whole channel's airtime, so the API reports them apart and nothing here adds them.

    Every heard copy is counted separately, flood repetitions included: on the one shared radio channel each retransmission occupies the medium on its own. The sums can therefore exceed the window they are measured over — an rx share of 130% is a fact about parallel traffic, not an overflow. Each radio senses the carrier only near itself, so two relays can put the same packet on air at the same moment out of each other's hearing; the collision or the blend happens somewhere neither of them can observe locally. That is the CSMA/CD paradox: carrier sense is local, the channel is global, and a local share capped at 100% would hide the only sign of a saturated mesh. Nothing in the pipeline clamps the ratio; the chart bar clamps at the card width and says "more than fits" when it does.

    The channel floor

    The rx share is what this antenna heard. A copy heard deep in a flood tree also proves the copies that came before it: a packet received with a four-entry path was put on air at least four times before it arrived, whether or not those earlier copies ever reached this antenna — some of them ran in parallel elsewhere, out of earshot. Each window therefore also carries rx_floor_ms and rx_floor_percent: the heard copies at their measured cost, plus one priced copy for every shallower depth of a heard tree that no heard copy carries. The shallower copies are priced from the deepest heard one — each hop adds a path entry to the payload, a path entry being 2 or 3 bytes depending on the packet's transport size, and a copy recorded with its own measured width is priced with that width rather than the fallback — and the modulation model does the rest.

    It is a floor, never a ceiling: parallel branches of the tree that no heard copy proves are additional and unprovable, so they stay out. A copy with no packet hash counts for itself alone: without an identity its repeats cannot be matched to a tree, and pricing an unprovable tree from an unknown would dress absence of evidence up as measurement.

    The sender share

    occupancy_senders ranks senders by airtime, every heard copy included, and the table adds up to the whole heard channel. Attribution is what the wire carries, not what a guess could supply:

    Row Identity from
    adverts the advertised node's full 32-byte key, which is the first bytes of the payload: MeshCore signs adverts rather than encrypting them, so an advert relayed on a transport channel still names its advertiser
    the bot itself its own full key, recorded at transmission time
    unattributed everything else: the packet types that name no origin on the wire, message traffic above all

    A two-byte routing prefix is never used as a sender: it belongs to several keys, and a wrong identity in a table read as evidence is worse than an admitted unknown. The advert rule has a size floor rather than only a hex-shape check, because the head bytes of any readable payload are hex-shaped and a made-up key would be read as a station; the floor is what the advert structure actually needs (key + signature + name). On live traffic 299 of the last 300 adverts carried a head key that resolves to a tracked contact. The reserved unattributed row is a bucket, not a station — it carries the airtime but is excluded from the talker count, which stays a count of identities this radio can name. Because every copy counts, the table's total can exceed the wall-clock span, exactly like the rx share does.

    One more honest source sits behind the unattributed row: this radio's own decryption. When the radio decrypts a message, the event names its author — the network authenticated that identity, the same proof class as an advert's signature — and when the message can be tied to a specific logged RF copy (a correlated row, never a fallback, see #80) and the author's key prefix completes to exactly one known contact, the copies of that packet whose airtime has not been written yet move out of the bucket, by packet hash. What has already been flushed stays unattributed: relabeling written rows would make the ledger claim a knowledge this station did not have at the time. So the bucket shrinks from the moment a message is understood onward, and never behind it. Undecryptable traffic — what this radio's keys do not open — stays in the bucket, named as unknown.

    The writer drains faster than the radio decrypts: the RF copy is recorded on the packet event, and the decryption that names the author can reach this listener seconds later. A tracked increment therefore waits in memory for attribution_grace_sec seconds (60 by default) past the newest copy heard under its packet identity before the flush writes it, and the shutdown flush closes that grace early so nothing stays behind. On MeshCore open channels the wait usually changes nothing: the channel key is shared and the delivery event the radio exposes names no author at all, so that anonymity is structural, not a gap in this measurement — the bucket is its honest ceiling. The grace exists for the traffic where an identity does exist: what this radio decrypts with its own keys.

    Each row also carries an advert slice: the part of that sender's airtime spent on ADVERT payloads, kept apart because adverts are what a station costs for being present on the mesh, not for talking — a repeater that floods adverts constantly racks up airtime without being chatty. A row written before the slice existed keeps it NULL, never 0: the split was not recorded for that traffic, and a 0% there would claim a station that never advertised. Once such a row is written again the slice counts from that write onward, so its stated share is a floor over the row's retained history. The page renders the share as a percent, and a dash where it is unknown.

    The emitter share: who occupies the channel

    occupancy_senders answers who WROTE the payload. occupancy_emitters answers the other question, and for channel occupancy the closer one: whose antenna put THIS copy on the air. MeshCore's flood routing answers it on the wire — every relay appends its node address to the path, the leading bytes of the relay's public key, 2 or 3 bytes depending on the packet's transport size — and the LAST entry is the station that carried the copy this radio heard. On sampled live traffic every path prefix resolved to a tracked repeater, and the last entry of a copy heard at −43 dBm resolved to a repeater a few metres from the antenna: the nearest relay is visible as an emitter even though it writes no payload of its own.

    Emitter label From
    the path's last node address (4 or 6 hex digits) a relayed copy — the last entry is its transmitter
    a full public key a copy that arrived with no path, the sender's own antenna; and a transmission the bot made, charged to its own key
    unattributed copies the wire names no one for: undecoded traffic, direct copies whose only sender bytes are truncated, and TRACE paths, whose entries are per-hop SNR hashes, not node addresses

    The label stays as the wire sent it — and only a flood path names relays at all. A direct packet carries no route info, and what the decoder hands over for one is its transport codes read as path-sized chunks: the capture path therefore derives the path-based emitter and entry-relay labels only for FLOOD and TRANSPORT_FLOOD route types (on live traffic one direct ACK's transport code 62016C reached the ledgers before this gate, and its two 6-char labels under 6201 froze that node's fold). The airtime is charged whatever the route type. The reader (top_emitters) turns a node address into a contact name only when exactly one tracked contact begins with those bytes; an ambiguous prefix stays bytes, because naming a prefix that could belong to several stations is a coin flip presented as a fact. A transmission is its own emitter, and an advert repeated by a relay spends the relay's presence budget too — the advert slice is kept per emitter exactly as it is per sender.

    The same relay can nonetheless stand in the ledger under two labels: MeshCore writes a node address 2 bytes wide in short packets and 3 bytes wide in extended ones, so one antenna appears as 6201 on some copies and 62010a on others. Two rows for one relay read as two emitters, so the reader folds the short label into the longer one — over the whole ledger, not just the page's rows: the wider partner is often the small row (on live traffic 237 relay copies sat under 6201 while its only possible wider address held 18, below the page the fold first saw), so the reader draws a wide pool before folding, and a prefix whose unique wider address is still not in the fetched rows is relabelled to it without summing an airtime that fetch never saw. It folds but only when the longer address is the only emitter address the ledger knows under that prefix, and the folded labels are listed in the row (merged_prefixes) rather than absorbed silently. With two longer addresses heard, the 2-byte copies cannot be told apart, and folding them into either would attribute copies to a station the wire did not name; the short row stays alone. The fold is a view rule: the ledger keeps the labels at the widths the wire sent them. The fold sees the whole ledger, not just the page: the reader fetches a wide pool before folding, and when the one wider address is known to the ledger but absent from the fetched rows entirely, the short row is relabelled to it — the prefix can only mean that address — without summing in the partner row's airtime, which that fetch never saw.

    The two ledgers hold the same copies once per fact each — carrier and emitter are two different stations — so they must never be summed: a sum would bill the channel twice. Their value is precisely that they disagree: a repeater near the antenna can occupy most of this channel without ever appearing in the senders table, because relaying carries other stations' payloads. occupancy_emitters.advert_ms is NOT NULL, unlike the senders' column: the split is known from this table's first write, so there is no legacy row for a zero to hide an unknown behind.

    The entry-relay share: where relayed copies entered the mesh

    The same flood path answers a third question per copy, read from its other end: the FIRST node address is the first relay that repeated the packet — the gateway through which the copy entered the mesh, as far as this radio can prove. occupancy_relays (migration 39) keeps that fact apart from the other two.

    It is important about what it is not. It is not the author: a multicast's routing header carries the channel's transport code and one address per relay, never the writing station's address, and the writer's own antenna usually sits behind the first relay, not equal to it. It is not the emitter either: that is the path's LAST entry, the antenna that carried the copy here. A one-hop path is the only case where the two are the same node. And a copy heard directly passed through no relay at all, so it belongs to no row here: entry_relay_label() returns None rather than inventing a hop the wire did not carry, and its airtime is already accounted by the emitter ledger. This table is therefore deliberately smaller than the other two, and absence from it is not silence.

    Entry-relay label From
    the path's first node address (4 or 6 hex digits) a relayed copy — the first entry is its gateway
    (no row) a copy heard directly: it entered nothing; and TRACE paths, whose entries are per-hop SNR hashes, not node addresses

    The label rules are the emitter's rules unchanged: the ledger stores the node address at the width the wire sent it, the reader resolves it to a contact name only when exactly one tracked contact begins with those bytes, and the 2-byte/3-byte fold applies with the same uniqueness condition (merged_prefixes names what was merged).

    Three ledgers, three facts about every relayed copy — writer, gateway, antenna — and one airtime charge each. They must never be summed with one another; their value is that they answer three different questions, and a gateway that carries everyone's traffic can top this table without ever speaking for itself.

    Per-period shares: the day tables

    occupancy_senders, occupancy_emitters and occupancy_relays are cumulative: each row answers "since first seen, within retention", and nothing can cut a cumulative total back to a window. A page whose history selector offers 24 h–90 d while its tables show those counters invites exactly that misreading, so the same samples also write three finer grains — occupancy_sender_days, occupancy_emitter_days and occupancy_relay_days, one row per station per UTC day — and the flush builds each cumulative row as the sum of the day rows it just wrote, so the two grains cannot drift apart. Any window is then a sum over whole days: window_cutoff_day aligns it to the day boundary, and the oldest day overlapping the window counts whole, because part of a day nobody measured cannot be priced.

    The day rows start the day the breakdown starts. Traffic already inside the cumulative tables before it cannot be distributed across days after the fact — inventing that distribution would fabricate per-day facts nobody recorded — so a long window understates traffic older than the breakdown, and the page says so rather than letting the number speak. A window with no day row for a station is that station absent from what this radio recorded in it, not a measured zero.

    Storage conventions

    Three rules that come from the dashboard rollups and are worth knowing before reading a chart:

    • A gap is not a zero. A bucket that held no packet is absent from the result, not 0.0. Rendering an absence as zero produces a cliff at the retention boundary that a reader interprets as an outage. A bucket that was observed and held nothing on air is a real zero and stays a zero — those are two different facts.
    • Sums and counts, never means. A stored mean cannot be re-aggregated: averaging averages loses the weights. Any window is rebuilt from sums.
    • Two clocks. Rolling windows use a monotonic clock, so an NTP correction or a clock-sync command cannot stretch or shrink a window. Buckets are stored as UTC epoch seconds, because that is what a reader queries.

    Percentiles use nearest-rank rather than interpolation. On a handful of samples the interpolated percentile under-reports the tail, and the tail — a burst — is the part anyone looks at. A percentile over 60-second buckets is not the same measurement as a percentile over the 10-second instant windows, and the API does not pretend otherwise.

    Live numbers

    The windows live in the bot process. The web viewer is a separate process and can only read what the writer left behind, so the snapshot is stored in bot_metadata under occupancy.live, at the writer's own flush cadence, once per batch — never on the packet path.

    The snapshot's age is part of the answer. Without an age, a quiet channel and a bot that stopped recording look identical. The reader marks the snapshot stale once it is older than twice the bucket size (at least 120 s).

    The local network view

    The airtime sums answer how much was on air. Each window also carries who and what this node heard:

    Figure How it is counted What it cannot claim
    talkers distinct rx senders inside the window whose packets carried an identity Who was talking but not heard from here; and the unattributed bucket, which is heard airtime, not a station.
    unnamed copies rx copies inside the window charged to the reserved unattributed sender bucket A station. It is heard airtime without a name, stated so the packet total reconciles with the talkers count.
    emitter addresses distinct emitter LABELS of the rx copies heard inside the window — the flood path's last node address, a full key for a direct hear Stations: one relay can stand under its 2-byte and its 3-byte address, and a prefix names a crowd as readily as a node. The unattributed bucket adds no label.
    repeats hashed packets heard ÷ unique packet hashes The full flood. Hashes cover only what this node heard, so the factor is a floor on the repetitions, not a census.
    copy cost of the hashed rx airtime (rx_hashed_ms), the slice spent on copies after the first one this station heard for that identity (rx_copy_ms) — each copy priced at its own modulation The network's first emission. The baseline is this station's hearing, bounded by copy_horizon_seconds: "first" means first heard inside a horizon, so a packet re-flooded more than a horizon apart counts as a new first occurrence. The two baselines differ by a bounded, per-identity offset, never by an invented zero. And it covers hashed airtime only: an unhashable packet cannot be matched to a copy of itself, so it sits outside the measure.
    avg hops mean path_length of the heard packets whose path was readable Transit vs local for packets whose path did not decode.
    channel floor heard copies plus the shallower copies each heard tree proves (see above) Parallel branches no heard copy proves. A floor, never a ceiling.

    The mesh reply states the first five as one line, in the reader's language, and each clause agrees with its own number: 1 talker, 1 unnamed copy, 1 emitter address / 1 expéditeur identifié, 1 copie sans identité, 1 adresse émettrice. The fr clause says expéditeurs identifiés and not émetteurs on purpose: in this feature émetteur names the station whose antenna carried a copy, which is a different fact from who wrote the payload — that label is what made a correct count read as a broken measurement when it sat next to a larger packet total. Three counts, three questions, never added together, and a count the window does not hold is left out rather than shown as 0.

    The repetition factor is what a flood router leaves behind: the same packet heard over several paths arrives several times with the same hash, so a window reading x2.5 was hearing the network repeat itself. The hop depth says how far the heard traffic came from — everything at 0 is a purely local pocket, a mean of 2 is traffic crossing the mesh.

    An unknown stays unknown, same rule as the airtime: a window that heard no hashable packet carries no repetition factor rather than x1.0 (that would claim a network without repeats from the absence of evidence), and a packet without path information is left out of the hop mean rather than averaged in as 0 hops. The copy share follows the same rule: copy_percent divides by the hashed airtime only — unhashable packets sit outside it — and is absent, not 0%, when no hashed packet was heard. A share of 0% is itself an answer: this station heard identities and none of them repeated.

    Two windows of the same length are also compared: each window carries previous — the equal-length window immediately before it, read from the same ring buffer — and a trend over it, but only while the buffer can cover both. A fresh start has no previous window, and a previous window with no samples is absent evidence, not a quiet baseline: both answer insufficient_history rather than turning a boot into a flood. And each window says what the buffer can prove about it: truncated is true when hashed traffic was heard but the ring buffer no longer reaches back over the whole window — the repeats and the copy cost are then bounds, and the mesh reply and the page mark them as such (≥, (bounded)) instead of dropping them.

    The copy cost is classified when the sample is recorded, while the writer's identity memory is warm, and rides along in the sample and the bucket as precomputed sums. Unlike the cardinalities, those sums re-aggregate: a reader adding buckets gets a correct sum of sums. That is why rx_hashed_ms and rx_copy_ms are the one part of the network view that is written to the buckets. Buckets written before the columns existed carry NULL, and a history period that spans them reports the copy share as unknown rather than as a ratio over part of the period — an invented average is as dishonest as an invented zero.

    These counts are deliberately not stored in the buckets — with the one exception of the copy cost, whose two sums do re-aggregate (see above). The buckets hold sums and counts because those re-aggregate across windows; cardinalities do not — the same talker in two buckets is one talker, and a reader summing the column would over-count exactly the busy senders the figure exists to rank. The network view therefore lives in the ring buffer and in the live snapshot, and its retention is the ring buffer's (max_samples), not the bucket retention.

    Recording happens on the packet path that fills packet_stream, so it runs when the web viewer's writer is running. Retention is the usual [Data_Retention] keys (occupancy_samples_retention_days, occupancy_senders_retention_days, occupancy_emitters_retention_days, occupancy_relays_retention_days) applied by the hourly cleanup scheduler; pruning is what makes a "last 30 days" chart actually mean 30 days. The three per-station ledgers age on the same rule so a reader comparing them is never handed a young table against an old one, and the day tables age on the same keys as their ledgers — a selected window cannot reach further back than the totals it describes.

    copy_horizon_seconds is the copy-cost baseline: an identity stays "already heard" that long, so copies of it inside the horizon are charged to the flood and a re-flood after it counts as a new first occurrence. It bounds both the writer's identity memory and the error the baseline can make — two occurrences of one identity more than a horizon apart are charged as two first copies, a constant offset per identity, never an invented zero.

    Reading it

    The Web Viewer page /airtime draws all of this — the windows, the history chart, the percentiles, the sender share, the emitter share and the entry-relay share — from the two routes below, which are what it reads. The mesh command airtime answers with three figures and nothing else: the day's peak (the worst bucket of the UTC day at that bucket's own capacity), the average share over the last hour, and the average over the UTC day so far — all of them the rx share this radio hears. The averages divide by the capacity elapsed, not by the buckets that happen to exist: the writer leaves an empty bucket absent, so a denominator of existing buckets would let silence flatter itself by counting only busy minutes. A share above 100 % is possible and is not clamped — parallel transmissions each cost their airtime. A day with no sample recorded answers "nothing recorded", never 0 %. The page is the whole picture, the command is the three numbers a reader actually asks for; the network view, the per-sender ledgers and the modulation behind the estimate stay on the page, where there is room for them. The command replies in telegraphic style, built to fit one channel body, counted in bytes because an accented reply overflows the wire before it overflows a character count. The budget is what the sender's own reply path reports: 155 minus the radio's name and ": ", and a further 10 when the reply leaves on a regional flood scope — a regional radio answers from a smaller body than the same radio answering globally. When the body runs out of room the reply travels in pieces through the splitter every long reply uses, so a cramped body still gets all three figures rather than half of one.

    GET /api/telemetry/occupancy — the live windows, the network view and the sender share:

    {
  "enabled": true,
  "age_seconds": 4.2,
  "stale": false,
  "sf": 11, "bw_khz": 125.0, "coding_rate": 8, "preamble": 8,
  "window_rx_percent": 6.4,
  "window_tx_percent": 0.7,
  "window_rx_floor_percent": 11.1,
  "window_distinct_senders": 3,
  "window_unattributed_packets": 12,
  "window_emitter_labels": 2,
  "window_dup_factor": 2.4,
  "window_avg_hops": 1.5,
  "window_copy_percent": 41.3,
  "window_truncated": false,
  "window_prev_rx_ms": 1980.4
}

    window_dup_factor and window_avg_hops are absent evidence rather than zero when no heard packet carried a hash or a path; in the text format they render as an empty value, not as 0. window_copy_percent follows the same rule — null when no hashed packet was heard — and window_truncated says when the repeats and the copy share are bounds rather than a census. window_prev_rx_ms is the rx airtime of the equal window one length back, null when the ring buffer does not reach that far: a monitoring agent comparing snapshots gets the trend without keeping state of its own.

    The top_senders rows carry the stored columns (sender, airtime_ms, packets, advert_ms, first_seen, last_seen) plus what the route resolves for the page: name and short for a full public key that the contact tracking knows, unattributed for the reserved bucket, and advert_percent — the slice as a percent of that sender's own airtime, null when the slice is unknown.

    The top_emitters rows carry the same shape for the emitter ledger: emitter (a label, not necessarily a key), the same stored columns, and the same resolved name, short, unattributed and advert_percent — with name set for a node address only when exactly one tracked contact begins with those bytes. The two lists are two facts about the same copies; they must never be summed.

    The top_relays rows carry that shape for the entry-relay ledger: relay is always a node-address prefix — a flood path never carries a full key, and a direct copy never reaches this table — so unattributed is never set here, and name follows the same one-match rule. A relay row's absence is not silence: direct copies belong to no row by construction. The three lists hold the same copies once per fact each — writer, gateway, antenna — and must never be summed with one another.

    All three lists follow the page's history selector when the request carries ?days=N (clamped to 1–90): the rows are then the day tables summed over that window, day-aligned, and the payload says which window it answers with "share_window_days". Without days the cumulative ledgers answer, as they always did, and no share_window_days key is present.

    Each row of the three lists also carries capacity_percent: that row's share of what the channel could physically carry over the selected period, rounded to one decimal. The spectrum is one shared channel and one radio receives one packet at a time, so the ceiling is one channel-second per elapsed second — the payload exposes it as "period_capacity_ms", next to "period_start_ts", the first moment of the range answered: in the period view it is the day-aligned window's start (the selector says "24 h" while the window covers whole UTC days — the page prints the span it actually answers for so the percent can be checked), and in the cumulative view the oldest recorded moment in the ledgers (each station's first_seen, the oldest day row, the oldest sample bucket). A row at 100% would be one station filling the channel alone; a table summing above 100% is oversubscription — transmissions running in parallel whose copies this antenna still paid for. The three tables price the same medium from three ends, so the same ceiling applies to all three and their percentages are directly comparable — while the ledgers themselves stay unsummed. The percent is taken after the prefix fold, so one antenna carries one figure for all its copies, and it is null — not 0 — when the period's elapsed time is unknown (no recorded moment anywhere in the cumulative view).

    Each table's header also sums its whole ledger for the period, exposed as "period_totals": airtime_ms, packets, stations, advert_percent and last_seen over every row of that ledger (day rows inside the window, or the whole cumulative table), plus the sum's own capacity_percent against the same ceiling. The sum is never just the page's slice — the entry count next to it says where the slice ends — and it is null (not 0) when the ledger holds no row for the period. advert_percent is null when the period mixes rows from before the advert split: a share over only the rows that know the split is an invented period figure.

    GET /api/telemetry/occupancy/history?days=7 — buckets as sums and counts, plus the nearest-rank percentiles. Only buckets that were written appear in buckets. Each bucket also carries rx_hashed_ms and rx_copy_ms — NULL for buckets written before that split was recorded — and the payload states the period's copy_percent (all or nothing: null when any bucket in the range predates the split, because a ratio over part of the period is an invented period figure). The payload also carries previous: the equal-length period before this one, in the same shape, kept as its own object so the reader can tell "nothing happened before" (the range was recorded and held nothing) from "nothing was recorded before" (the range predates retention). The /airtime page draws the comparison from it and states a change only when both periods hold recorded buckets.

    Both accept ?format=text, which renders one field per line for a monitoring agent. Only scalar fields are rendered that way; the bucket list and the sender list are not, so read them as JSON. Both routes are on the anonymous allowlist, like the storage probe: the payload carries aggregate counters and sender keys that GET /api/contacts already shows.

    enabled is the setting as the reader knows it, not the value the snapshot happened to be written under: recording can have been on when the snapshot was written and off now, which is two facts. The viewer re-reads enabled from config.ini on every request and only falls back to the copy it read at boot when the file cannot be read — a hot config reload never reaches the running viewer process, and a page that tells the reader to set the flag has to answer from the file the reader would edit. When the setting and the snapshot disagree the payload also carries snapshot_enabled, and the page says the same thing on screen — the setting is off now, and the figures shown were written while it was on, with the age saying how long ago. A request that fails is not evidence about the setting either, so the page takes back its claim rather than leaving it standing beside the error.

    Passive airtime analysis

    The /airtime curve, duty-cycle estimates and modal detail tables are fed by a second, passive read model — modules/airtime_analysis.py and its companions (airtime_capture, airtime_forwarding, airtime_quality, airtime_repetitions), adopted from the Baliz fork (Camille Ballon). It reads the same packet_stream the writer already fills and maintains its own airtime_observations table: one row per distinct packet plus capture-continuity intervals, so silence is recorded as silence rather than as the absence of a row.

    It answers different questions from the three ledgers above. It reconstructs, per distinct packet, who authored it and which relay retransmissions the flood history implies, so it can estimate a repeater's duty cycle ("reconstructed" and "scenario" percentages against the 10 % reference, 6 min/h) — something the ledgers, which price each heard copy at its own airtime, never claim. The reconstruction is an estimate: no inference here proves duty-cycle compliance, and the payload carries its schema_version and method.version so a reader can tell which method produced a number. GET /api/telemetry/occupancy/analysis?window=1h|24h|7d serves it; unknowns are null, never 0.

    The two read models are never summed: the analysis counts distinct packets and reconstructed relays over rolling windows (1h/24h/7d), the ledgers count heard copies per fact over whole UTC days. The /airtime page draws only the analysis; the ledgers stay recorded and stay served by GET /api/telemetry/occupancy (its top_senders, top_relays, top_emitters and period_totals), but nothing on the page renders them — see the page section below.

    The /airtime page

    The page is the Baliz design: French throughout, times displayed in Europe/Paris, unknowns shown as N/D, one chart (channel-occupation bars plus packets/min) up front, short top-five lists, and the station-ranking tables inside a modal. The period selector (Dernière heure, Dernières 24 heures, 7 derniers jours) drives every card; a silence marker is drawn only where the recorder proves a complete window, so a gap never reads as a quiet channel.

    Every ranking of stations lives in the modal, over the analysis read model: its role tabs (authors, entry relays, emitters) and the "Voir tous" folds answer the same question the three by-airtime ledgers used to answer on this page, so the ledger card was retired rather than kept beside tables of the same stations under a second read model. The ledgers themselves — recorded daily, day-aligned, with the day's occupancy command reply built from them — are unchanged and still readable through GET /api/telemetry/occupancy; only their rendering left the page.

    The page keeps one rounding: every number is shown to at most two decimals, because a third decimal claims a precision the modelled sums do not carry and mixed rounding across cards reads as two different measurements of one fact. A non-zero average that would round to 0 at two decimals is shown below the floor (< 0,005), never as 0. The page also still fetches GET /api/telemetry/occupancy?limit=1 for one field only: whether the recorder in this process is on. The analysis read model does not know that, and a historical page has to say so rather than look live.

    What this can be used for

    • Deciding whether a mesh is actually busy, or whether a channel looks busy only from where you sit.
    • Judging a bot's own contribution — a talker sending many short packets costs more airtime than one sending a long message, which is why the sender share is ranked by airtime and not by packet count.
    • Setting a fairness limit, if one is ever added. A limit has to be built on airtime, because a limit built on message count rewards short messages and punishes long ones.

    It cannot tell you whether the channel was clear, whether a packet was collided, or whether a repeater was hearing you. For that you need a repeater's own counters.
